theia Rust · P2P overlay + services

A public-key-addressed peer-to-peer overlay and services layer I architected and built. You reach a machine by its ed25519 key instead of its address, and every service behind that reach is gated by a capability rooted in the same key, verified offline with no coordinator. The networking underneath is pluggable: iroh today (its hole-punching does the NAT traversal) and quirk, a UDP stack I wrote from scratch to understand how a modern transport works; by design the reach layer extends to a purpose-built transport per medium (Bluetooth and other non-IP radios), which is a goal, not yet built. A shared conformance suite holds every transport to one identity contract: a transport that misattributes who it reached is rejected, and a reference transport each must pass keeps the interface from overfitting to iroh. It's a stack of small Rust crates:

  • bifrost (reach): opens a byte-stream to a machine named only by its ed25519 key, carried over a swappable transport. Finding the peer across NATs is the transport's job (iroh's hole-punching); you address who a peer is, not where.
  • nauthy (the gate): one ed25519 key mints, narrows, delegates, and revokes capability tokens, each verified offline with no coordinator, rooted in the key a transport handshake already proved. Attenuable, expiring, and revocable through a denylist the node checks at admission.
  • tightbeam (the tunnel): publishes your local services by name behind the gate, runs a short handshake that selects one and confirms admission before a byte flows, then hands back a plain two-way stream, so any program speaking a TCP port or Unix socket rides it unchanged.
  • services (the engines): standalone units, fetch, measure, sshh (keyless SSH), and transfer, that each do one job on an already-admitted stream, embeddable one at a time.
  • swoosh (the product): one CLI that binds the layers into human verbs, ssh in, send files, forward ports, fetch through a peer, hand out revocable access, all by public key, no IPs or servers in the middle.

Approach

I build systems where control sits with the person using them, not with a company in the middle. In the theia-hq overlay, your identity is a key you hold, not a row in someone's database, so there is no account to register, no dashboard to suspend, and nothing anyone can deplatform. I reach for the smallest design that does the job, which sometimes means writing a layer from scratch to understand it and then deliberately not shipping it. The test I hold work to is whether it still answers to you when the vendor, the server, and the network in the middle are all gone.
